Tune the real sky.

The radio tunes from 10 MHz radio waves through infrared and visible light to X-rays and gamma rays, and at every frequency you see the whole sky as it really looks there. The tapes on the shelf hold real sounds recorded on Mars, near Jupiter and at a launch pad on Earth.

Best with headphones.

A radio for the sky, and a shelf of tapes

Every position on this dial is a real frequency, and the map above it is the entire sky at that frequency. The galactic center sits in the middle; the bright band is the disk of the Milky Way, seen from inside.

Up to 5 THz, the maps come from GSM2016, a model by Haoxuan Zheng and colleagues that combines 29 maps of the sky, from ground-based radio surveys to the Planck satellite, into one continuous radio sky.

Above that, the dial leaves radio behind. Each station is a real all-sky survey, from the IRAS satellite's infrared to Fermi's gamma rays, with Axel Mellinger's photograph of the whole night sky in the middle. Between surveys you get static: no telescope has mapped the whole sky at those frequencies.

How to tune

  • Drag the dial, or roll the tuning wheel on the side of the player. Your scroll wheel makes small moves.
  • Click a station's name on the dial to jump to it. With the player on Radio, the rewind and fast-forward keys jump between stations, and Band steps through the spectrum.
  • On a keyboard: left and right fine-tune (hold Shift for bigger steps), up and down jump between stations, M mutes, and the space bar plays or stops a tape.
  • Point at the sky to read its temperature at that spot (radio maps only).
  • Share a frequency or a tape: the address bar always holds the one you're on.

Built the Arduino tuning knob from the README? Plug it in and connect it here. This works in Chrome and Edge.

The tapes

Every tape is a real recording, published by NASA on its Sounds from Beyond page. On Mars they come from two places. InSight's seismometer felt vibrations rather than sound in the air, so most of its recordings were sped up for human ears. Perseverance carried the first microphones to record sound on another planet. The Juno tape is radio, shifted down into hearing range. Where NASA released two versions of a recording, they are the two sides of one tape.

While a tape plays, the sky marks where that planet was, seen from Earth, on the day it was recorded, worked out from JPL's tables of the planets' orbits. The player is an original design: its reels turn the way a cassette's would at 4.76 cm a second, and its counter follows the recording itself.

What's real here

The maps, temperatures and wavelengths are data. On the radio half of the dial, the loudness of the static follows the median brightness temperature of the whole sky at the frequency you're on, cosmic microwave background included.

Temperatures are Rayleigh–Jeans brightness temperatures, the convention radio astronomers use. It's why warm dust near 3 THz reads in millionths of a kelvin: at those frequencies, even warm things look faint on this scale.

The survey images are the surveys' own pictures, as published by the CDS archive in Strasbourg, reprojected here into the same map. Their colors are the survey teams' (or, for single-band surveys, a tint added here); visible light is true color.

Station sounds are translations. Spectral lines and survey colors are slowed by whole octaves, so their pitch ratios stay true; the pulsar keeps its real period; GPS plays its real satellite code, slowed down. Jupiter and the FM band are synthesized stand-ins, not recordings.

The band labels on the dial are a simplified version of the US Table of Frequency Allocations. The clear slits are bands where, under footnote US246, no station may transmit, so astronomers can listen.
